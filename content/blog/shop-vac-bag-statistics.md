- The **median disposable bag costs $6.00** across the five common 16 gallon bags we priced.

Crews on fine dust spend more than the table above, because they change bags daily. From the jobs behind Muk Buddy (Floor Lord Industries field data):

- That is roughly **$200 per crew per month** on bags.
- A **5-crew operation** can spend around **$12,000 a year** on bags alone.
- The average crew burns about **$1,000 per vac per year** on disposables.

## Dust rules that affect shop vac filters

Silica dust from concrete, stone, brick and tile is regulated, and the rules name the filter a vacuum must use.

- **50 micrograms per cubic meter:** OSHA's permissible exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica, averaged over an 8-hour shift (29 CFR 1926.1153).
- **25 micrograms per cubic meter:** the action level that triggers exposure monitoring (29 CFR 1926.1153).
- **99% or greater:** the filter efficiency OSHA's Table 1 requires for the dust collector on many tools, such as handheld grinders and drills, along with a filter-cleaning mechanism (29 CFR 1926.1153, Table 1).
- **99.97% at 0.3 micrometers:** the efficiency a filter must reach to count as HEPA under the same standard.
- **No dry sweeping** where it adds to silica exposure, unless wet sweeping, HEPA-filtered vacuuming or other low-exposure methods are not feasible (29 CFR 1926.1153).
- **2.3 million workers, 2 million in construction:** OSHA's estimate of US workers exposed to silica. OSHA projected the rule would save more than 600 lives and prevent more than 900 new silicosis cases a year (OSHA final rule announcement, 2016).
- **HEPA vacuum for lead dust:** EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires a HEPA vacuum for cleanup when renovating homes built before 1978 (40 CFR 745, subpart E).

A bag is not a HEPA filter. Its job is to keep fines off the filter so the filter can do its job. See [HEPA filters explained](/blog/hepa-filters-explained/) and [best shop vacuum for concrete dust](/blog/best-shop-vacuum-concrete-dust/).
