## The subscription nobody signed up for

Here's something worth noticing: the vac was sold to you once, but the bags are sold to you forever.

That's not an accident. Disposable shop vac bags work on the same razor-and-blades model as printer ink and coffee pods. Sell the machine cheap, make the money back on consumables you buy over and over. Nobody calls it a subscription, but that's what it is: a recurring cost you didn't agree to and can't easily opt out of.

A crew on fine dust can burn two or three bags a week. At $5 to $13 per bag, that's roughly $500 to $2,000 a year, per vac, every year. The vac itself might have cost $150. The bags cost more than the machine, and they never stop.

## Why it works better (not just cheaper)

The cost math is easy to see. The performance difference is the part people miss until they've lived it.

A disposable bag sends dust and air through the same path. As it fills, fine drywall or concrete dust packs against the filter media and chokes airflow. Suction drops. The motor works harder against the clog, runs hotter, and dies sooner. By lunch, the bag is "full" even though it's barely loaded with debris.

A reusable bag with a 2-chamber design works differently. Debris drops into the first chamber. Airflow passes through the second. The filter stays clean, suction stays steady, and the motor isn't straining against choked air. That's what keeps a vac pulling through a full shift.

So the choice isn't just "$99 once versus $500 a year." It's also "suction that holds versus suction that dies."
